\title{Does In-Domain Masked Autoencoding Overtake ImageNet Initialisation Under Label Scarcity?\\
Two Pre-Registered Studies Across Four Visual Domains}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Namrata Pathak}
\IEEEauthorblockA{M.Tech in Artificial Intelligence and Data Science\\
KIIT University, Bhubaneswar, India\\
namratapathak0410@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
For label-poor domains it is often suggested to skip ImageNet and pretrain on the domain's own unlabelled images.
We tested this for masked autoencoders (MAE) and a small Vision Transformer (ViT): as labels grow, when does a ViT
pretrained with in-domain MAE beat the same ViT started from ImageNet-1k weights, and does distance from ImageNet
tell us in advance? Two pre-registered studies covered four domains (plant disease, satellite, objects, animals),
leakage-safe splits, label budgets from 5 to 100\,\% and three seeds on one laptop GPU. In Study~1 (240 runs) the
ImageNet ViT won all 20 dataset--budget cells. The gap closed steadily with more labels but never reversed (on
PlantVillage from $-0.239$ to $-0.020$ macro-F1), and none of four domain distances predicted it (exact
$p \geq 0.33$). Study~2 (405 runs) removed three possible handicaps. A four times larger unlabelled pool did not
improve MAE (all differences within $\pm 0.02$), a wider learning-rate grid left ImageNet ahead in all 20 cells,
and ResNet-34 behaved like ResNet-18. Continuing MAE from the ImageNet weights was never significantly better than
ImageNet alone and was significantly worse in 10 of 20 cells. At this scale, ImageNet initialisation is the safer
default.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Outside everyday photography, labels are usually the costly part of a vision project. Anyone can photograph a
tomato leaf, but naming its disease takes a plant pathologist, and satellite or medical images have the same
problem. The usual remedy is to start from weights learned on ImageNet~\cite{deng2009imagenet}, 1.28 million
labelled photographs of everyday objects.

Self-supervised learning offers another route. A masked autoencoder (MAE)~\cite{he2022mae} hides most of an image
and learns to fill the gaps back in, so it needs no labels and can be trained on exactly the images one cares about.
The trade-off is plenty of knowledge from the wrong visual world against a little from the right one. MAE on small
target datasets has been reported to help
ViTs~\cite{elnouby2021largescale,zhou2022selfpretrain,mao2022maedatahungry,das2024limited}, and in-domain
contrastive pretraining can rival ImageNet in some settings~\cite{cole2022contrastive}. We could not find an answer
to the practical question of \emph{at what label budget} the in-domain route starts to pay off, or whether this can
be read off from how unlike ImageNet a domain is, a link that Ericsson et~al.~\cite{ericsson2021transfer} leave open.

We address this with two pre-registered studies. Our contributions are: (1) a comparison of four starting points on
four domains, in which the three ViTs share one architecture and differ only in their initial weights; (2) a
crossover analysis over five label budgets, showing that in-domain MAE never overtakes ImageNet although the gap
shrinks in every domain; (3) four domain distances measured before training, which disagree with each other and
do not predict the gap; (4) a follow-up that removes three possible handicaps of MAE and tests a hybrid that
continues MAE from ImageNet weights, none of which changes the conclusion; and (5) evidence that accuracy rankings
replicate across domains while calibration rankings do not. Both studies were committed to a public repository
before their first training run and took about 36\,h on a laptop GPU in total.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Transfer from ImageNet.} Stronger ImageNet models tend to transfer better~\cite{kornblith2019transfer},
yet for medical images much of what ImageNet offers turns out to be unnecessary~\cite{raghu2019transfusion}. ViTs
\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} have no built-in locality, unlike CNNs~\cite{he2016resnet}, and depend heavily on
pretraining; DeiT~\cite{touvron2021deit} showed they can be trained on ImageNet-1k alone.

\textbf{In-domain and continued pretraining.} The value of self-supervised features depends on the target
domain~\cite{ericsson2021transfer}, and both the amount and the domain of pretraining data matter for contrastive
models~\cite{cole2022contrastive}. Masked pretraining on the target data alone can be
competitive~\cite{elnouby2021largescale,zhou2022selfpretrain} and reduces the data needs of
ViTs~\cite{mao2022maedatahungry,das2024limited}; the diversity of the pretraining set also plays a
part~\cite{hammoud2024diversity}. Continuing pretraining on in-domain data is common in language
modelling~\cite{gururangan2020dontstop}, and Reed et~al.~\cite{reed2022hpt} report gains when self-supervised
training starts from ImageNet weights. For plants, CNNs reach high accuracy on
PlantVillage~\cite{hughes2015plantvillage,mohanty2016plant}, and self-supervised transformers have been trained on
plant images~\cite{wang2024gpid}. None of these locates the label budget at which in-domain MAE overtakes ImageNet
across several domains.

\textbf{Distances and calibration.} FID~\cite{heusel2017fid} compares feature distributions, and
OTDD~\cite{alvarezmelis2020otdd} adds the labels. Temperature scaling~\cite{guo2017calibration} is the standard fix
for overconfidence, and calibration has been revisited for newer models~\cite{minderer2021calibration} and for small
medical datasets~\cite{sambyal2023calibration}. Since results on one benchmark often fail to carry
over~\cite{dehghani2021lottery}, we use several domains and rank statistics~\cite{demsar2006comparisons}.

\subsection{Arms}
All arms are fine-tuned on the same labelled subsets (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Study~1 has four: \textbf{A}, a
ResNet-18~\cite{he2016resnet} from random weights (11.2\,M parameters); \textbf{C}, a ViT-Tiny in the DeiT-Tiny
layout~\cite{touvron2021deit} (12 blocks, width 192, 5.5\,M parameters) with supervised ImageNet-1k weights;
\textbf{D}, the same ViT from random weights; and \textbf{E}, the same ViT initialised by an MAE trained on the
domain's own training images without labels. As C, D and E share one architecture, their differences come only from
the starting weights. E$-$C is the main contrast; E$-$D shows what MAE adds, and E$-$A compares it with a CNN.
Study~2 adds \textbf{E2}, which is E with a four times larger unlabelled pool; \textbf{F}, which takes C's
ImageNet weights, continues MAE on that pool and is then fine-tuned; and \textbf{A2}, a ResNet-34 (21.3\,M
parameters). Model sizes and the 160\,px resolution were chosen so that each study fits within 48\,h on one
RTX~4050 Laptop GPU (6\,GB).

\subsection{Data, splits and label budgets}
Each domain is one dataset (Table~\ref{tab:data}): PlantVillage~\cite{hughes2015plantvillage} (leaf diseases),
EuroSAT RGB~\cite{helber2019eurosat} (land cover), CIFAR-10~\cite{krizhevsky2009cifar} (objects) and Oxford-IIIT
Pets~\cite{parkhi2012pets} (cat and dog breeds). We draw 7,000 images per dataset, stratified by class, resize the
short side to 160\,px and crop the centre. Near-copies, such as repeated shots of one leaf, would inflate test
scores if they straddled a split, so we hash each image (256-bit difference hash), group images whose hashes differ
in at most 10 bits, and keep every group inside one split of a 70/15/15 \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} split. The
label budgets are 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100\,\% of each class's training images (at least one per class), i.e.\ about
250 to 5,000 labels. Smaller budgets are subsets of larger ones, and every arm sees the same subset for a given
seed (0, 1 or 2), which also fixes data order, new-layer initialisation and E's MAE run.

\subsection{Distance from ImageNet}
Images are embedded with C's frozen ImageNet ViT (192-d class token) and compared with ImageNetV2 (matched
frequency, 10,000 images)~\cite{recht2019imagenetv2} as a stand-in for ImageNet. We measure (i)
FID~\cite{heusel2017fid}; (ii) OTDD~\cite{alvarezmelis2020otdd}, whose matching cost also reflects class similarity
(diagonal Gaussian per class, 64 PCA dimensions, 1,500 samples per side, POT solver~\cite{flamary2021pot}); (iii)
ImageNet confidence, the mean top-class probability of the ImageNet classifier on the domain (0.458 on ImageNetV2
itself); and (iv) the validation accuracy of a linear probe on the frozen features. Higher FID or OTDD and lower
confidence or probe accuracy mean ``farther away''.

\subsection{MAE pretraining}
The MAE encoder is the same ViT-Tiny, so its weights load directly into the fine-tuned model. It hides 75\,\% of
the 100 patches, and a small decoder (2 blocks, width 128) predicts the normalised pixels of the hidden ones. We use
batch 256, AdamW ($\beta_2 = 0.95$), learning rate $1.5\times10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.05, cosine decay, random
resized crops and flips, with sine--cosine position embeddings at the start. For E the pool is the $\sim$5,000
training images, seen for 800 epochs (15,200 steps, $\sim$21.5\,min); each seed gets its own MAE.
In Study~2 the pool holds 20,000 images: the training images plus 15,000 more drawn from \emph{outside} the
7,000-image sample, so validation and test images cannot enter. Extras whose hash is within 10 bits of a validation
or test image, or of a flipped copy, are dropped as well (227 on EuroSAT, none elsewhere). E2 keeps E's 15,200
steps, so only the data changes. F uses the same schedule from the ImageNet weights, keeps their position
embeddings and lowers the learning rate to $5\times10^{-5}$ to avoid erasing them; this value was fixed in advance,
not tuned. Oxford Pets has no further images, so it has no E2 and its F uses the 5,000 training images.

\subsection{Fine-tuning and statistics}
All arms follow Table~\ref{tab:setup}. Validation macro-F1 is checked 25 times per run; training stops after 8
checks without gain and the best weights are restored before the single test evaluation. Pretrained ViTs use
layer-wise learning-rate decay of 0.75~\cite{bao2022beit}. The learning rate per dataset and arm is chosen on
validation macro-F1 at the 20\,\% budget with seed 0 and reused everywhere. Study~2 widens the grid to five values
and retunes every arm; old runs are reused where the chosen rate is unchanged and repeated where it changed.

The main metric is test macro-F1; we also report the expected calibration error (ECE, 15
bins~\cite{naeini2015ece}) before and after temperature scaling fitted on validation data~\cite{guo2017calibration}.
Gaps between arms are paired by seed and given with 95\,\% $t$-intervals. The crossover budget is the sign change
of the mean gap on a log-budget axis; without one it is reported as censored ($>$100\,\%), and it counts only if
the intervals exclude zero on both sides. Each dataset's gap is summarised by its average over log-budget, which we
relate to each distance by Spearman's $\rho$ with an exact permutation $p$ and a leave-one-dataset-out prediction.
With four datasets no two-sided $p$ can fall below 0.083, so this analysis was declared exploratory. Ranking
agreement across datasets uses Kendall's $W$~\cite{kendall1939rankings} and the Friedman test.

\section{Study 1: Results}
All 240 runs finished in 14.6\,h (9.3\,h fine-tuning, 1.0\,h learning-rate selection, 4.3\,h MAE), using at most
3.4\,GB of GPU memory.

\textbf{No crossover.} C had the best mean macro-F1 in all 20 dataset--budget cells (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves},
Table~\ref{tab:f1}). E$-$C was negative everywhere and its 95\,\% interval excluded zero in every cell, so the
crossover is censored at $>$100\,\% for all datasets. H1 holds only in a weak form: the gap closes with more
labels in every domain but never flips. On PlantVillage it shrank from $-0.239$ at 5\,\% to $-0.020$ at 100\,\%
(CI $[-0.037, -0.003]$) and on EuroSAT from $-0.183$ to $-0.049$, while CIFAR-10 ($-0.443$ to $-0.196$) and Oxford
Pets ($-0.620$ to $-0.252$) kept a wide gap.

\textbf{Distance does not predict the gap.} Averaged over budgets, the gap was much smaller for PlantVillage
($-0.118$) and EuroSAT ($-0.110$) than for CIFAR-10 ($-0.317$) and Pets ($-0.489$), whose objects and animals
ImageNet covers well; on Pets the ImageNet classifier is even more confident (0.51) than on ImageNetV2 (0.46). Yet
the four distances disagree on which domain is far away (Table~\ref{tab:data}): FID puts PlantVillage farthest,
confidence puts EuroSAT farthest and OTDD puts EuroSAT \emph{closest}. FID ($\rho = +0.6$) and confidence
($\rho = -0.8$) point the way H2 expected, OTDD the other way ($\rho = -0.4$), and the linear probe not at all
($\rho = 0$). No exact $p$ is below 0.33, and only confidence predicts a held-out dataset better than the mean
(error 0.124 against 0.193). H2 is not supported.

\textbf{MAE versus scratch.} E beat the scratch ViT in all 20 cells, significantly in 13 (H3 mostly supported).
The gain was largest on Pets ($+0.353$ at 100\,\%, 0.638 against 0.286) and CIFAR-10 ($+0.111$ to $+0.171$), and
small and never significant on EuroSAT ($+0.007$ to $+0.037$), where the scratch ViT was already good. Against the
scratch ResNet-18, E stayed between $-0.020$ and $+0.055$, significant in only 2 of 20 cells. At this scale MAE
gives a ViT about what its convolutional design gives a ResNet.

\textbf{Rankings and calibration.} The order of the arms by macro-F1 (C, then E and A, then D) was nearly identical
across domains: Kendall's $W$ was 0.90--1.00 at every budget (Friedman $p \leq 0.013$), so H4 is rejected.
Before temperature scaling C also had the lowest ECE in 17 of 20 cells (mean 0.052, against 0.119 for E, 0.154 for D
and 0.171 for A). Scaling brought every arm to 0.024--0.032 on average, after which the ranking no longer agreed
across datasets ($W = 0.05$--$0.53$, all $p \geq 0.098$), and the scratch ViT was the best calibrated in 7 cells.
H5 is partly supported.

\textbf{Wider learning-rate grid.} With five candidate rates the choice changed for 9 of the 16 Study~1
dataset--arm pairs, and those 135 runs were repeated. Nothing important moved: C still won all 20 cells among
A, C, D and E (H11 supported), and E$-$C stayed negative everywhere, significantly in 19 cells. The closest case is
again PlantVillage at full labels, now $-0.012$ instead of $-0.020$.

\textbf{Four times more unlabelled images.} If MAE had simply been short of data, a larger pool should have helped.
It did not: E2 and E differed by at most 0.02 in any cell, E2 led in 8 of 15 and no difference was significant
(H6 not supported). E2 stayed below C in all 15 cells, 14 significantly, and came no closer than 0.014
(PlantVillage, 100\,\%; H7 not supported). Even the reconstruction loss barely changed (0.407 against 0.409 on
PlantVillage, 0.266 against 0.268 on CIFAR-10).

\textbf{ImageNet first, then MAE.} The hybrid F beat C in only 3 of 20 cells, by at most 0.006 and never
significantly, and was significantly worse in 10 (H8 not supported). The harm is concentrated at small budgets: at
5\,\% F trailed C by 0.351 on CIFAR-10 and 0.542 on Pets, most of ImageNet's original lead over in-domain MAE. With
all labels it came back to within 0.04 of C everywhere. F was well ahead of E2 (0.021 to 0.210, significant in 14
of 15 cells; H9 supported), but that edge comes from the ImageNet start. F also stopped early in only 17\,\% of its
runs, close to E (20\,\%) and far from C (67\,\%).

\textbf{A larger ResNet.} ResNet-34 and ResNet-18 were within 0.03 in 18 of 20 cells; the one significant
difference was CIFAR-10 at 100\,\% ($+0.042$). The verdict of MAE against a scratch CNN changed in a single cell (H10
partly supported). Mean ECE before scaling was 0.052 for C, 0.116 for E2, 0.146 for F, 0.151 for E and 0.176 for
ResNet-34; after scaling all arms fell between 0.024 and 0.033, as in Study~1.

\section{Discussion}
\textbf{Why ImageNet keeps winning.} ImageNet-1k has about 250 times more images than Study~1's pool and 64 times
more than Study~2's, and its labels force the network to learn shapes, parts and textures that prove useful even for
leaves and satellite tiles. MAE learns by repainting pixels. Its reconstructions (Fig.~\ref{fig:recon}) recover the
layout, such as a leaf's outline and colour, but blur fine detail, including the lesions that separate many
PlantVillage classes. With enough labels, fine-tuning can learn that detail, which fits a gap that shrinks with
the budget. That four times more images left both the reconstruction loss and the accuracy unchanged suggests the
limit lies in what pixel reconstruction teaches at this model size rather than in how many images it sees. MAE is
usually trained on far larger collections~\cite{he2022mae}, so our result means ``four times more does not help'',
not ``more can never help''.

\textbf{Why continued MAE hurt.} Continuing with MAE seems to pull the ImageNet network away from the class-level
features that made C strong and towards features suited to repainting pixels. With few labels there is too little
signal to pull it back, so the damage is largest at 5--20\,\% and almost gone at 100\,\%. Reed
et~al.~\cite{reed2022hpt} saw the opposite, but they started from self-supervised weights and used contrastive
objectives. Our learning rate for F was also a fixed guess, and F ended with a higher reconstruction loss than MAE
from scratch (0.456 against 0.407 on PlantVillage), so a gentler or shorter schedule may behave differently. This
simple recipe, at least, is not a free improvement.

\textbf{Where in-domain MAE comes closest.} The gap was smallest for PlantVillage and EuroSAT, the domains least
like ImageNet's object photos, as H2 intended, but no distance captured it; one number seems too blunt to forecast a
crossover. EuroSAT also had by far the lowest reconstruction loss (0.19, against 0.27--0.54 elsewhere) and the
smallest gain over a scratch ViT, so a low reconstruction loss is no sign of a useful representation.

\textbf{Practical advice.} With up to twenty thousand unlabelled images and a small ViT, start from ImageNet at
every label budget. In domains unlike ImageNet its lead at full labels can be small (about one point on
PlantVillage), and in-domain MAE is still a clear step up from training a ViT from scratch. Calibration should be
checked per domain, since the accuracy ranking does not carry over to it.

% =====================================================================================================
\section{Limitations}
The studies were sized for a laptop GPU. (i) ViT-Tiny, 160\,px and at most 20,000 unlabelled images per domain;
larger models, resolutions and pools favour MAE~\cite{he2022mae}, so a crossover may exist beyond our range. (ii)
Four domains cap any rank correlation at $p \geq 0.083$, and three seeds leave wide intervals for small effects.
(iii) Even on the wider grid, 16 of 27 chosen learning rates lie at an end of the grid; since widening it barely
changed the results, we do not expect further tuning to reverse them. (iv) One MAE recipe was used, and F's MAE
learning rate was not tuned. (v) DeiT weights were trained at 224\,px and interpolated to 160\,px, and CIFAR-10 and
EuroSAT were upsampled. (vi) Hashing cannot catch every near-duplicate, such as a leaf photographed from another
angle. (vii) ImageNetV2 replaces ImageNet in the distances, Pets had no extra images for E2, and compute was not
equalised between arms.

% =====================================================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
Over four domains and two pre-registered studies, in-domain MAE did not overtake ImageNet initialisation of a small
ViT anywhere between 5 and 100\,\% of the labels. ImageNet's lead shrank as labels were added and was smallest for
plant-disease and satellite images, but no standard distance predicted where it would be small. Four times more
unlabelled data, a wider learning-rate grid and a deeper ResNet baseline did not change this, and continuing MAE
from ImageNet weights hurt when labels were scarce. In-domain MAE did reliably improve a ViT trained from scratch
and matched a CNN trained from scratch. Much larger unlabelled pools, larger ViTs and more domains are the natural
next steps, and would also allow the link between domain distance and the crossover to be tested properly.
